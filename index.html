Check your understanding

Three questions that pull the steps together. Answer first, then test with the model.

Which spreadsheet columns add up to ΔH(T), and which to TΔS(T)?

ΔH(T) = F + I = ΔHref + ΔCp(T − Tref). TΔS(T) = H + J = TΔSref + TΔCpln(T/Tref). The spreadsheet's ΔG = F − H + I − J is ΔH(T) − TΔS(T).

Two proteins have the same Tm and ΔHm, but protein B has a larger ΔCp. Which is more stable at 25 °C?

Protein A. The ΔCp term, ΔCp[(T − Tm) − T·ln(T/Tm)], is never positive and is zero only at Tm, so a larger ΔCp pulls the curve further down on both sides of Tm. For the class protein, doubling ΔCp takes ΔG at 25 °C from about 33 to about −10 kJ/mol. Try it: pin the curve, then raise ΔCp.

How is the two-state unfolding model like all-or-none ligand binding?

Both treat the change as one cooperative step with no populated intermediates. A molecule is either N or U, just as P either has no ligand or all n ligands (PLn). That cooperativity is what makes the unfolding transition sharp, as all-or-none binding makes the binding curve sigmoid.

Model. Two-state equilibrium N ⇌ U. ΔG is the free energy of unfolding (GU − GN), so positive ΔG means the folded state is favored. ΔCp is assumed independent of temperature. K = [U]/[N] = exp(−ΔG/RT), R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).
Molecule animation. Each molecule flips at random so that, on average, the fraction folded matches equilibrium. The flip speed is for display only; it is not real folding kinetics.
Units. This page converts °C to K with 273.15; the spreadsheet uses 273. Between 0 and 100 °C this changes ΔG by less than 0.02 kJ/mol. ΔSref = ΔHref/Tref has units of kJ/(mol·K).
